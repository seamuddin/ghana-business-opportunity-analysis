# Industry Analysis (Porter's Five Forces and Value Chains)

> **Purpose:** Explain *why* profits in each shortlisted industry are high or low, and where a small entrant can capture value. Force intensity is scored **1 (weak force, favourable to the entrant) to 5 (strong force, unfavourable)**, with evidence for each score. Evidence labels and source IDs as in [data/sources.md](../data/sources.md).

## 2. Solar PV installation (residential and SME)

**Value chain:** Chinese and other manufacturers → Accra importers and distributors (product registration with the Energy Commission, REG-24) → installers and online retailers → customers, increasingly with **bank financing** (ENE-46, ENE-47).
**Where the margin sits:** Importers earn a volume margin. **Installers earn the trust and service margin.** That is why certified installers charge GHS 55–85k for a 5 kVA system (ENE-44), while marketplace kits sell for GHS 25–37k (ENE-18).

| Force | Intensity | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | **4** | Anyone can resell kits online (ENE-18). Licensing exists, but the Energy Commission had to order defaulting firms to renew (ENE-19) | Differentiate on certification and warranty, which casual entrants cannot copy cheaply |
| Supplier power | **3** | Many brands and importers, but all priced off USD; FX swings pass straight through (MAC-07) | Approve two distributors per component; buy only against customer deposits |
| Buyer power | **3** | Online price lists make prices transparent (ENE-17, ENE-43), but buyers cannot judge technical quality | Sell outcomes (hours of backup, warranty), not components |
| Threat of substitutes | **3** | Petrol generators, inverter-only backup, PAYG systems (ENE-21); grid upgrades planned (ENE-06) | Target customers where outages cost money (SMEs) |
| Competitive rivalry | **3** | Fragmented market; price competition at the low end; few service-led mid-market players with public prices | Mid-market positioning with transparent packages |
| **Average** | **3.2** | | **Moderately attractive** |

## 3. Laundry and dry cleaning

**Value chain:** Equipment and detergent suppliers + utilities (ECG, Ghana Water) → laundry processors → pickup/delivery logistics → households and institutions.
**Where the margin sits:** In **processing efficiency** (utilisation of machines and labour) and **customer retention** (subscriptions, B2B contracts). Logistics is now a cost of doing business: free pickup is standard (SVC-34).

| Force | Intensity | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | **4** | Low capital; informal home-based washers everywhere | Build brand on reliability; lock in customers with subscriptions |
| Supplier power | **3** | Consumables are commoditised, but utilities are regulated monopolies with quarterly tariff resets (ENE-09–12, SVC-37) | Water storage, generator, night-time heavy loads |
| Buyer power | **3** | Low switching costs; visible price lists (SVC-32–35) | Loyalty through turnaround guarantees and garment care |
| Threat of substitutes | **4** | Washing at home; house helps | Target time-poor professionals and institutions, not price-sensitive households |
| Competitive rivalry | **3** | A handful of organised players (FUA Xpress, Laundry Chief, Wash N Go, JN) plus informal operators | Neighbourhood density beats city-wide reach |
| **Average** | **3.4** | | **Moderate** |

## 4. Broiler production

**Value chain:** Grand-parent/parent stock and hatcheries → day-old chicks (GHS 20–21, AGR-09, AGR-10) → feed millers (De Heus, cooperatives; AGR-11, AGR-31) → farms → live-bird markets, processors, chop bars → consumers. Imported frozen chicken competes at the retail end (AGR-01, AGR-12).
**Where the margin sits:** With **feed millers and hatcheries** (concentrated suppliers) and with **importers** (scale). Grow-out farms are squeezed between both.

| Force | Intensity | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | **3** | Government programme distributing birds to ~60,000 households (AGR-05) adds subsidised capacity | Avoid commodity live-bird markets crowded by programme flocks |
| Supplier power | **4** | Feed is ~70% of production cost (AGR-13); few large feed millers and hatcheries | Buy maize at harvest; mix own feed with concentrate; long-term supply agreements |
| Buyer power | **4** | Distributors want local chicken within ~10% of import price (AGR-34); 2026 glut (AGR-06, AGR-36) | Sell direct to chop bars and festive buyers; pre-sell batches |
| Threat of substitutes | **4** | Frozen chicken ≈ GHS 33–40/kg vs local ≈ GHS 49/kg (AGR-12, AGR-33); fish and other proteins | Compete on freshness, not price |
| Competitive rivalry | **4** | Imports (82% of supply, AGR-03), integrated farms (AGR-29), thousands of small farms | Scale is a disadvantage at US$20k |
| **Average** | **3.8** | | **Unattractive at small scale** |

## 5. Last-mile delivery for online sellers

**Value chain:** Online sellers (Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok, Jumia vendors) → delivery platforms or courier fleets → riders → customers. Cash-on-delivery money flows back to sellers (ENE-53, ENE-54).
**Where the margin sits:** Platforms take a commission. Fleet operators earn on **route density** (drops per bike per day) and **reliability**, which sellers value because failed deliveries and slow COD remittance cost them customers.

| Force | Intensity | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | **4** | A bike and a phone are enough; work-and-pay bike schemes (ENE-56). New licensing and union rules raise the bar slightly (ENE-32–34) | Compliance becomes a selling point for business clients |
| Supplier power | **2** | Bikes and parts widely available (ENE-35); fuel price regulated by NPA (MAC-18) | Standardise on one bike brand |
| Buyer power | **4** | Sellers use several platforms at once; price transparency in apps (ENE-51, ENE-52) | Contracted, scheduled service with service-level guarantees |
| Threat of substitutes | **3** | Sellers deliver themselves; customer pick-up | Make COD remittance and tracking the reason to switch |
| Competitive rivalry | **4** | Bolt Send, Yango, Swoove, informal riders (ENE-50–52); Glovo's exit shows thin margins (ENE-28) | Niche B2B focus, not consumer on-demand |
| **Average** | **3.4** | | **Moderate** |

## 6. SME digital-commerce services

**Value chain:** Platforms (Meta, Google, TikTok, payment gateways at 1.95%, ENE-61, ENE-62) → agencies and freelancers → SMEs → SMEs' customers.
**Where the margin sits:** With the **platforms**. Service providers earn a labour margin unless they productise their work or sell measurable outcomes.

| Force | Intensity | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | **5** | Only a laptop and skills needed; freelancers from US$350/month (snippet) | Specialise by vertical to build reputation quickly |
| Supplier power | **3** | Platforms control reach, ad prices and features | Diversify channels; avoid dependence on one platform |
| Buyer power | **4** | Micro-firms are price-sensitive and can do it themselves | Low-cost entry packages; results-based fees |
| Threat of substitutes | **4** | DIY tools, marketplace storefronts (Jiji, Jumia), Bolt listings | Bundle set-up + payments + delivery integration |
| Competitive rivalry | **4** | Many agencies with public price lists (ENE-57–60) | Price below agency entry tiers |
| **Average** | **4.0** | | **Unattractive industry structure, offset by very low capital needs** |

## 7. Cross-industry comparison

| Industry | New entrants | Suppliers | Buyers | Substitutes | Rivalry | **Average (lower = better)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solar installation | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | **3.2** |
| Laundry service | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | **3.4** |
| Delivery fleet | 4 | 2 | 4 | 3 | 4 | **3.4** |
| Broiler farm | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | **3.8** |
| Digital services | 5 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 | **4.0** |

### Conclusions

1. **Solar installation has the most favourable industry structure.** Information asymmetry protects trusted installers from pure price competition, and demand is growing faster than supply of certified installers.
2. **Broiler production faces strong forces on both sides:** powerful feed and chick suppliers, and buyers anchored to cheap imports. This confirms the low profitability seen in the financial model and the PESTLE finding of a net-negative environment.
3. **Digital services is structurally the least attractive industry.** Its overall ranking (#2) is driven by low capital at risk, not by industry economics. An investor choosing it is buying a cheap option on proving demand, not a defensible position.
4. Across all five, **threat of entry is high (4–5)**. None of these businesses can rely on barriers to entry. Sustainable advantage must come from **execution quality**: reliability, trust, compliance and customer relationships.
